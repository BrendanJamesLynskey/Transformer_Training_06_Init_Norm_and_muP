01

The Scale of the Initial Weights

Before the first step every weight is a random number, and how large those numbers are decides whether the signal and the gradient survive the trip through many layers. A matrix whose entries have standard deviation \(\sigma\) multiplies a vector of \(d\) numbers into one about \(\sigma\sqrt d\) times as large.

Xavier / He: \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt d\)

Keeps the size the same layer after layer. For \(d = 16\): 0.25.

GPT-2: \(\sigma = 0.02\)

Part 1’s choice (Radford et al. 2019), far smaller here: every sublayer starts out adding almost nothing. GPT-2 also scales residual layers’ weights by \(1/\sqrt N\) for \(N\) residual layers, because every block adds to the same stream.

02

Pre-LN and Post-LN

Pre-LN (Part 1’s design, GPT-2 onwards)

$$x_{l+1} = x_l + F_l(\operatorname{LN}(x_l))$$ The sublayer reads a normalised copy; the stream itself is only ever added to.

Post-LN (the original Transformer)

$$x_{l+1} = \operatorname{LN}(x_l + F_l(x_l))$$ Every block normalises the sum, so every gradient must pass through the LayerNorms above it.

The experiment: an 8-block version of Part 1’s model at initialisation, on “alice was”, with weights at std 0.02 and at std 0.25: the RMS of the residual stream entering each block, and the norm of each block’s weight gradients (results.md §7a).

03

Through Depth: the Residual Stream

An 8-block version of Part 1’s model at initialisation on “alice was” (results.md §7a).

With std 0.02 every sublayer adds almost nothing: the pre-LN stream is essentially the embedding plus the positional encoding, RMS 0.70 at every depth. With std 0.25 the pre-LN stream grows with depth, from 0.72 entering block 1 to 4.30 leaving block 8, because each block adds to it. Post-LN normalises after every addition, so its stream stays at RMS 1.

04

Through Depth: the Gradients

Weight-gradient norm per block at initialisation, std 0.25 (results.md §7a).

Post-LN pays for its tidy stream in the gradients: at initialisation the blocks nearest the output get the largest (post-LN 1.29 at block 1, 2.29 at block 8). Xiong et al. (2020) proved this for post-LN at initialisation and showed it is why post-LN transformers need a learning-rate warmup: a full-size step on those large gradients makes training unstable. In pre-LN the residual path carries the gradient back unchanged (deck 03’s highway).

Pre-LN trains with or without warmup. Post-LN without warmup is still on the plateau in every seed; with warmup, some runs start learning, not all. This is why GPT-2 and nearly every large model since put the norm first.

06

RMSNorm

LayerNorm subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, then scales and shifts. RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich 2019) skips the mean and the shift:

$$y = \gamma \odot \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac1d \sum_i x_i^2 + \epsilon}}$$
NormParametersFinal validation loss (mean ± sd)Seeds 1, 2, 3
LayerNorm5,3762.277 ± 0.0902.299, 2.354, 2.178
RMSNorm5,2962.140 ± 0.0082.148, 2.132, 2.141

Cheaper and, in practice, as good; LLaMA uses it, as pre-normalisation (Touvron et al. 2023, §2.2). On Part 1’s model (the default run, three seeds) RMSNorm even ends lower with fewer parameters, but LayerNorm’s spread over the seeds is wide: read it as “at least as good” (results.md §7c).

07

Why the Learning Rate Does Not Transfer Across Width

Tuning the learning rate of a large model directly costs several large runs. One would like to tune it on a narrow model and reuse it on a wide one. Under the standard parameterisation (SP) that does not work.

The reason

With Adam each weight moves by about the learning rate per step (deck 04). A wider matrix sums more of those moves into each output, so the same learning rate is a bigger effective step for a wider model, and the best rate shifts down as the width grows.

The maximal update parameterisation (µP; Yang et al. 2022) rescales the initialisation, the learning rate and a few multipliers with the width so that each step changes every layer’s activations by about the same amount at any width. Then the best rate found on a narrow model carries over (“µTransfer”).

What µP changes for Adam, relative to a base width \(d_0\) (here 16, Part 1’s) with \(m = d/d_0\): hidden matrices start at std \(0.02/\sqrt m\) and their learning rate is divided by \(m\); attention scores are scaled by \(1/d_k\) instead of \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\); the logits are divided by \(m\) (Yang et al., Table 3).

08

µP and the Coordinate Check

Coordinate check, results.md §7d. Flat lines mean width-independent updates.

The coordinate check: 10 AdamW steps at learning rate 0.01 for widths 16 to 256, and how far the logits move (RMS change, on 16 fixed windows; results.md §7d). Under SP they move 1.43 at width 16 and 4.48 at width 256; the last residual stream moves 124. Under µP the logits move 1.43 and 1.42: width-independent, which is the whole point.

10

What to Take Away

  • The initial weight scale sets how signals and gradients grow through depth.
  • Pre-LN lets the stream grow but keeps gradients even; post-LN keeps the stream at 1 but skews gradients towards the output and needs warmup. Measured: post-LN without warmup never left the plateau.
  • RMSNorm drops the mean and shift: cheaper, at least as good here.
  • Under SP the best learning rate shifts with width; µP makes updates width-independent so a narrow model’s rate transfers.
